Here is what nobody told you.

This is almost never about disobedience.

Studies in Nigerian primary schools found bedwetting in roughly 7 to 23 out of every 100 children, depending on the city.

In Port Harcourt, it was nearly 1 in 4 pupils aged 6 to 12.

Doctors describe three things that can be involved. Here they are, in pictures you already know.

The first picture is deep sleep.

Picture a house at night. The doorbell is his bladder. If the person who should answer is in a very deep sleep, the bell rings and nobody comes.

That is why he can wet the bed and not know it happened.

The second picture is how much urine his body makes at night.

Think of a bucket under a tap. If the tap runs faster at night, the bucket fills before morning, even though nothing is wrong with the bucket. Late drinks and a heavy supper can make the tap run faster.

The third picture is how his bladder behaves.

Now picture a small bucket and a big bucket under the same tap. The small one overflows first. Some children’s bladders hold less, or squeeze suddenly.

Nigerian studies also linked it to family history, heavy late suppers, snoring, and being hard to wake.

It is common, and it is not his fault.

Think of a child learning to ride a bicycle. He wobbles, he falls, and then one day it clicks. The body learns in its own time, and a steady hand beside him helps.

Doctors’ guidance says it becomes less common as children grow, and that help is available at any age up to 19.

Many families see a change when they sort out drinks, supper time, toilet habits, and the words they use.
